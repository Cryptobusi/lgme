The mission, inside the rules.

This page is a record of changes. It is not a lawyer’s letter, not a tax opinion, and not a determination by the Internal Revenue Service or any state.

What the model requires

Hederica is the mission frame for the whole desk. The floor stays senior. The tape stays visible. A hidden print is still refused. The vote, when one is described, is a human act, not the size of a holding.

Speculative profit is not the purpose. The service to be built is a public record of civic work: time given, a deed attested, an hour that cannot be quietly rewritten. That record is not a mint, not a bank, and not a sale of tokens.

A charitable or social-welfare exemption is the aim, not a present fact. A 501(c)(3) must serve a public purpose and must not let earnings inure to insiders. A members-only exchange of private benefit is a poor fit for that statute and may belong in a cooperative, or nowhere, until counsel chooses. A 501(c)(4) gift is generally not deductible. Neither status is claimed here.

Changes made

  1. Hederica now says the exemption is not claimed. No board is seated. No wage is paid. An hour is not cash, a deposit, a security, or a token for sale. A Hedera timestamp records a deed and does not move money.
  2. Gifts, where an account is named, are personal. They are not a charitable contribution, not deductible, and they buy nothing. No fiscal sponsor is named. See disclosures.
  3. The seigniorage note is labeled an archive. It stays readable. It is not the live mission and not a solicitation to venture capital or to buyers.
  4. Tokens named on these pages stay references to other people’s instruments, or to design research. These pages do not issue them and do not sell them.
  5. The floor, the tape, and the refusal are unchanged. Compliance did not add a new mint.

What was left in place

The essays, the whitepaper, and the October 2026 seigniorage note remain, marked as research. Deleting a source would hide the record. A reader can still open them. A reader should not treat them as an offer.

Recommended next

  1. Counsel chooses one vehicle before any public fundraising. A charitable timebank for a charitable class can seek 501(c)(3) recognition, or a fiscal sponsor. A private exchange among members should not wear a charity’s name. Do not file both stories at once.
  2. Do not solicit as a charity, and do not say a gift is deductible, until a determination letter or a named fiscal sponsor exists. Many states also require charitable-solicitation registration before the ask.
  3. Seat an independent board before any wage. Write a conflict-of-interest policy and a compensation policy. Reasonable pay, approved in advance, with the comparison written down. On a future 501(c)(3), excess-benefit rules apply to insiders.
  4. Write the hour rules before any balance exists: one person, one hour; not sold; not bearing interest; not redeemable for cash; not transferable to strangers; no custody of dollars or tokens by the site. Have a money-transmitter reading before any hour can be cashed out. A timestamp is not a payment.
  5. Keep securities out. Do not sell a token, a share, or an expectation of profit from the work of others.
  6. Privacy on a public ledger: consent, the smallest record that still proves the deed, no sensitive categories, a pseudonym where the law allows.
  7. Separate volunteers from the wage. People giving hours are not employees because a ledger noticed them. A future wage is employment, and it is not paid in hours.
  8. Books, insurance, and a public annual note, even before exemption. An unpublished change is still not a change.
