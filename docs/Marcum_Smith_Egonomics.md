# Marcum & Smith Egonomics — Ego as Asset or Liability

## Citation

Marcum, David, and Steven B. Smith. 2007/2008. *Egonomics: What Makes Ego Our Greatest Asset (or Most Expensive Liability)*. New York: Fireside / Simon & Schuster.

## Summary for incorporation into lgme

Ego is neither pure asset nor pure liability. It supplies drive, confidence, and tenacity, but becomes an expensive P&L line when unmanaged. Marcum and Smith identify four early warning signs that ego has shifted from asset to liability:

1. Being comparative (too competitive makes you less competitive)
2. Being defensive (defending ideas becomes defending yourself)
3. Showcasing brilliance (intelligence and talent crowd out better ideas)
4. Seeking acceptance (desire for respect/recognition interferes with judgment)

The three balancing principles that keep ego in the asset column are:

- **Humility** — opens minds (not meekness; can coexist with intensity)
- **Curiosity** — systematically tests assumptions
- **Veracity** — makes the undiscussable discussable and closes the gap between perception and reality

In banking and credit contexts the authors note that senior executives escalate commitment to problem loans precisely because they approved them (ego + sunk-cost). The same dynamic appears whenever an issuer or agent defends a past print, widens a residual, or resists a visible correction.

## Application to the lgme order

Floor-first seniority, public mint, named charge/risk before residual, titled remainder (including zero), and “an unpublished change is not a change” already function as structural humility and veracity. They force the present self to confront the book rather than protect its own prior decision. Curiosity is practiced when every line must be readable in multiple tongues (person, conversion, charge, basket) and a missing tongue writes nothing.

Together with Schelling’s precommitment barriers, the Marcum/Smith principles keep the third seat from collapsing into ego defense of the print.
