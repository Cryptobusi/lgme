Time bank governance models

Simple summary. Time bank governance is usually light and membership-driven, built around equal-hour mutual credit. Models range from horizontal assemblies to hosted or coordinator-led structures. It works as organized barter for social reciprocity but faces classic limits: coordinator dependence, soft rule enforcement, and scale constraints. These map to the same frictions seen in other complementary circuits.

This note records the public exchange. It is not a transcript of every word, and it is not advice.

Core principles and credit mechanics

Edgar Cahn’s model rests on equal valuation (one hour of any service = one time credit), reciprocity, redefining work to include care and community tasks, and the idea that everyone has assets to contribute. Credits are mutual credit: a transaction credits the provider and debits the recipient so the system sums to zero. There is no central issuer, no interest, and usually no legal enforceability—only a social obligation. Credits cannot be sold for fiat. Matching is handled by a broker or software directory.

Common governance models

Community-led / organic. Members set rules through general assemblies or membership meetings (majority or consensus). An open coordination group or rotating stewards handles matching, onboarding, and disputes. Examples include Helsinki Timebank and some Greek time banks that stress direct democracy.

Hosted / program-sponsored. A host organization (clinic, school, NGO, local government) provides the platform, sets criteria, and often employs a coordinator. Credits may still circulate, but core rules are shaped by the host’s goals. This improves stability and funding at the cost of member control.

Hybrid with explicit roles. Structures such as Street Works Time Bank use stewards and co-organizers who earn time credits for stewardship. A written policy book covers participation, values, inactivity, and limited sanctions (e.g., unanimous steward decision to pause membership). Many U.S. and UK time banks sit in this middle ground.

A minority register as formal nonprofits with boards. Experimental blockchain versions with token-driven voting exist in research but remain rare. Most still rely on conventional ledgers and human brokers.

Decision-making, disputes, and limits

Day-to-day operations fall to coordinators or stewards. Strategic changes go to assemblies or host leadership. Disputes are handled by mediation or referral to principles. Membership requires accepting core principles; inactivity rules are common. High early failure rates are documented (one European note cited roughly 70% failing within three years). Recurring issues include coordinator burnout, funding for administration, inactive balances, and the tension between horizontal ideals and practical hierarchy.

Time banks deliberately differ from LETS by using time rather than a negotiable value unit and by prioritizing social capital over local commercial circulation. This simplifies valuation but leaves liquidity dependent on the density of offers.

Relation to egonomic and barter themes

Time banks already use proactive, attested credit and mutual-credit mechanics that loosen the pure double coincidence of wants. Their governance is usually softer than Schelling-style precommitment and a constitutive tape. Rules can be restated without a durable barrier that treats unpublished changes as non-changes. A third-seat-style refusal right is often implicit but rarely formalized against coordinators or hosts. Adding floor-like seniority for core principles, slower amendment paths, and an immutable record of rule changes would address capture and drift while preserving the egalitarian hour unit. Hosted models gain stability at the cost of sovereignty; pure assembly models preserve sovereignty at the cost of continuity.

Barter, exchange, and named credit

Simple summary. IdidIt-style credit creation on lgme.app sits closer to modern organized barter (trade credits inside a network) than to a general monetary exchange. It loosens the double coincidence of wants inside the network without becoming general money. Effects on the wider economy stay limited. Complementary currencies have a long history of exactly this pattern; WIR Bank is the durable Swiss example of a closed-circuit, bank-issued credit that clears among members.

Barter versus exchange

Barter is the exchange of goods or services of roughly equal worth without fiat money. Modern barter exchanges use an internal unit (trade credits) governed by a third party. Exchange is the broader term that includes both barter and currency systems. The primary difference is that ordinary exchange allows money; pure barter does not. Barter is itself a form of exchange.

IdidIt credit creation

lgme.app records acts (goods, time, skills, civic work) on a public tape and can generate named credit that is explicitly not cash, not a deposit, and not a token offered for sale. Settlement options treat tokens as a named promise, not cash at the register. Floor-first rules, public schedule, and visible tape govern issuance. This matches the structure of a modern barter exchange with trade credits more closely than a monetary system in which any seller accepts the unit from any buyer.

Double coincidence of wants

Pure one-to-one barter requires that each party simultaneously wants what the other offers. Organized barter networks and named-credit systems relax this by letting a participant earn credit for one thing and later claim something else inside the network. Matching and valuation frictions remain; the credit is not general purchasing power.

Implications for trade

Asynchronous multi-party matching becomes possible. Previously unrecorded small acts can become visible. Cash can be conserved. Volume stays constrained by network scope, liquidity, and acceptance. Trade favors reciprocal, community-scale, or mutual-aid exchanges and disfavors broad impersonal commerce.

Effect on the rest of the economy

Limited and mostly local. The credits do not expand the broad money supply, do not alter the general price level, and do not interfere with monetary-policy transmission. They can mobilize some idle local resources and conserve fiat for participants, but they remain a parallel restricted circuit.

Complementary currency history

Parallel units have recurred whenever official money was scarce or poorly suited to local needs. Examples include Josiah Warren’s Time Store (1827), Owen’s Labour Exchange (1832), Depression-era scrip, Wörgl (1932), WIR Bank (1934–), Japanese and U.S. time banks (from 1973/1980s), LETS (1983), Ithaca Hours (1991), and Argentine trueque networks. Common pattern: restricted circulation, crisis or niche activation, limited scale, complement rather than substitute for national money.

WIR Bank mutual credit mechanics

Founded 1934 in Switzerland as a cooperative response to credit scarcity. Issues electronic CHW (WIR franc), pegged 1:1 to CHF, accepted only inside the network. New CHW enters mainly when the bank itself extends collateralized credit to members. Subsequent trades clear among members. Price parity is required. Early demurrage was abandoned. Account balances earn no interest; loans can be cheaper. It is a closed-circuit, bank-mediated system rather than pure peer-to-peer mutual credit, and it has remained stable for nine decades as a small complement to the Swiss franc.
