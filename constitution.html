Executive Summary

As peer-to-peer protocols, automated cryptographic verifiers, and local artificial intelligence models challenge traditional jurisdictional oversight, questions emerge regarding how foundational constitutional law protects decentralized systems.

This brief details how the United States Constitution interacts with Sovereign Provenance Protocols (SPP), local cryptographic verification pipelines, and Artificial Intelligence agents categorized under dual-use or munitions statutes.

Section I: The U.S. Constitution and Distributed Provenance Protocols

The Sovereign Provenance Protocol (SPP)—grounded in deterministic intake, Ed25519 signatures, frequency-domain watermarks, and immutable public ledger anchoring—derives direct legal viability from multiple constitutional provisions, including the Contract Clause, First Amendment protection of code as speech, and Fourth Amendment protection of digital papers and effects.

Intelligence Compact — Digital Arms & the Second Amendment

The Constitutional Ontology of Digital Arms (September 2026)

Doctrinal analysis of whether encryption, vulnerability scanners, AI agents, and cyber weapons can satisfy the historical and textual definition of “arms.” Purely digital software faces a high bar under the “bearable” requirement. Dual-use tools are more plausibly protected under the First Amendment or Fourth Amendment. Offensive cyber capabilities are likely “dangerous and unusual.”

Fourth Amendment as a Practical Shield

Defensive cybersecurity measures are more readily protected under the Fourth Amendment (as secure digital property / “papers and effects”) than under the Second. Local AI models, encryption keys, and private vaults can be characterized as digital papers and effects requiring a warrant for seizure.
