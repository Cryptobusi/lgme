Related Resources: AI, Digital Arms & Constitutional Protections

A selection of analyses examining whether software, AI agents, cyber tools, or dual-use technologies can or should fall under Second Amendment protections. Most mainstream DC think-tank work remains focused on the First Amendment; the pieces below are among the few that directly engage the “software-as-arms” question. The Fourth Amendment is also relevant as a more practical shield for local defensive tools.

Intelligence Compact — Digital Arms & the Second Amendment

The Constitutional Ontology of Digital Arms: A Second Amendment Analysis of Cyber Weapons, AI Agents, and Autonomous Systems (September 2026)

Doctrinal analysis of whether encryption, vulnerability scanners, AI agents, and cyber weapons can satisfy the historical and textual definition of “arms.” Key points:

  • Current doctrine requires arms to be “bearable” and used for offensive or defensive physical confrontation (Heller).
  • Purely digital software faces a high bar because it is intangible and often wielded remotely, not carried on the person for kinetic conflict.
  • Dual-use tools (penetration-testing software, encryption) are more plausibly protected under the First Amendment as expressive code or under property/Fourth Amendment doctrines.
  • Offensive cyber capabilities capable of critical-infrastructure damage are likely “dangerous and unusual” and therefore outside Second Amendment coverage.
  • The strongest constitutional shield for digital defensive tools remains the First Amendment (Bernstein), not the Second.

Fourth Amendment as a Practical Shield

The Intelligence Compact analysis notes that defensive cybersecurity measures are more readily protected under the Fourth Amendment (as secure digital property / “papers and effects”) than under the Second. Local AI models, encryption keys, defensive agents, and private vaults can be characterized as digital papers and effects. The government generally cannot seize or compel disclosure of them without a warrant and probable cause. This supplies a realistic protection for tools that help individuals defend their own systems without needing to clear the high Second Amendment hurdles of physical bearability.

Additional Articles

  • OSD 384: AI is the frontier of the Second Amendment — Open Source Defense (July 2026). Argues that as technology grows more dangerous, the Second Amendment’s logic of decentralized access is the better framework than heavy regulation.
  • The Second Amendment and Cyber Weapons — Jan Kallberg (arXiv, 2018). Early examination of whether individuals have a Second Amendment right to own military-grade cyber tools.
  • The Right to Bear Technology: America’s Other Second Amendment — Katherine Boyle / a16z (October 2025). Broader claim that the Second Amendment is the only amendment that explicitly protects a specific technology.
  • Future Weapons Are Already Protected by Second Amendment — Bearing Arms (December 2025). Applies Heller/Caetano logic to future and non-traditional arms, including directed-energy and autonomous systems.
