A public book for work the banks never counted.

The aim is a 501(c)(3) or 501(c)(4) mutual-benefit protocol: civic timebanking and micro-mutual credit in place of a speculative mint. The blind spot is real. Informal human capital — a neighbor helped, a lesson given, a walkway cleared — never reaches a balance sheet, because the old rails cost more to clear than the act is worth.

Under that aim the service is a public good. Members would exchange labor and good deeds on an honor-backed ledger. A wage, if one is ever paid, would be a reasonable W-2 set in advance by an independent board. It would not be a claim on other people’s hours, and this site pays no wage today.

No exemption is claimed. There is no determination letter, no seated board, and no fiscal sponsor. An hour here is not cash, not a deposit, not a security, and not a token offered for sale. A Hedera timestamp records a deed. It does not move money. The charter lists what changed, and what counsel still has to do.

1. The core thesis: tokenizing lost, unrecorded value

In conventional economies, vast amounts of real wealth are permanently lost because the administrative and verification costs exceed the face value of the act:

  • Helping an elderly neighbor move groceries
  • Forty-five minutes of unbilled peer chemistry tutoring in a dorm
  • Clearing snow off a community walkway
  • Mentoring a freshman or organizing a campus cleanup

Because traditional banking rails demand $0.30 + 2.9% per swipe and require contracts, invoices, and accounting departments, these high-value societal contributions vanish from balance sheets.

By leveraging Hedera Consensus Service (HCS), each authenticated good deed or micro-service is inscribed for $0.0001 per consensus timestamp, creating an auditable, immutable ledger of community sweat equity that costs practically nothing to notarize.
